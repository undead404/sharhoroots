# Find researchable article gaps

Review the specified article (or the article attached to the request), identify unanswered questions that could add meaningful value for genealogists or historians, create research-task notes for the worthwhile ones, and apply worthwhile navigation links directly in the article. This is a task-triage pass, not substantive source research or article rewriting: navigation-only edits are in scope.

## Workflow

1. **Resolve the target.** Use the supplied repository-relative article path. If none is supplied, use the attached article. If there is no unambiguous article, ask which one; do not guess. Confirm it exists and read it in full, including frontmatter and headings.
2. **Understand its scope and entity type.** Identify the reader-facing question, entity, period, and kinds of claims the article is meant to cover. Use its path and frontmatter (such as `type: Place`, `PlaceOfWorship`, or `Organization`) and its actual subject to choose relevant coverage checks below; do not force every check onto every article. Read directly linked notes and nearby relevant context only as needed to understand the article and avoid suggesting something it already answers. Do not turn this into a broad vault audit.
3. **Check the research queue.** Read `tasks/TEMPLATE.md` and search `tasks/` for semantically overlapping tasks, not just matching filenames. Check tasks in every state so completed, blocked, or `no_result` work is not recreated as if new. An existing task may be reported as a match; do not silently edit or reopen it.
4. **Check type-specific coverage as well as textual clues.** A useful gap may be entirely absent, so do not limit the audit to questions explicitly raised in the prose. Compare the article with the relevant prompts below, its own stated scope, and nearby articles of the same type. Treat these as questions to assess, not mandatory fields to fill mechanically:
   - **Settlements and places:** historical names and their dates; administrative affiliations over time; constituent or dependent settlements where relevant; associated parishes and record coverage; important local institutions, communities, families, and people.
   - **Administrative divisions:** when the unit existed; its administrative level, center, and parent unit; which settlements it covered, with dated evidence; and predecessor/successor units or boundary changes where useful.
   - **Churches, parishes, and other places of worship:** settlements served and changes in the catchment over time; denomination/rite and institutional affiliations; available registers and their date ranges/gaps; documented operating history and building status (for example active, closed, repurposed, abandoned, or demolished), with dates and evidence where known; and notable clergy or congregants.
   - **Organizations and institutions:** purpose and period of operation; area or population served; predecessor/successor or related entities; relevant records; and people whose roles or documented connections illuminate its history.
   - **People connected to any entity:** check for notable or locally significant people and families with a sourced, meaningful connection—such as residents, founders, owners, clergy, officials, teachers, or community leaders—and for existing person notes that should be linked. “Famous” is not a required quota: do not invent a notable connection, imply kinship from a shared name, or create a task merely because no famous person is mentioned.

   For each absent or thin area, ask whether it matters to genealogical or historical readers and whether a plausible research lead exists in the article, linked notes, repository source records, or relevant source collections already identified in the vault. Create tasks only for worthwhile answerable questions, not to satisfy a checklist.

5. **Mine article-specific research leads.** Do a separate pass over the article's own evidence and finding aids before deciding which gaps merit tasks. Inspect its citations, named entities, linked-note leads, and any tables or lists of archival fonds, files, collections, events, or people. Such material can suggest valuable questions that a generic type checklist will miss.
   - Extract candidate directions from what is actually named or described: for example, a record title may point toward family records, elections, migration, public health, land, labor, education, infrastructure, or an institution's work. Treat each as a lead to assess, not as evidence of the record's contents or of an event having occurred.
   - Group related rows into meaningful themes before drafting tasks. Do not create one task per archive row, repeat near-identical questions for each fund, or turn a large inventory into a task backlog. For a long article or extensive inventory, cluster the clues by topic, period, population, or source type and assess the strongest distinct directions; use representative clues where multiple entries point to the same question.
   - Look beyond the first obvious gap: compare the candidate directions from this pass with the coverage review and consider whether the article offers materially different research perspectives. There is no required number of themes or tasks, and breadth is not a quota; stop when remaining candidates are duplicative, weakly grounded, outside scope, or low-value.
   - Keep separate (a) what the article establishes, (b) what its cited or listed material might help investigate, and (c) what remains unknown. An archive title is a clue, not confirmation of contents, names, events, or outcomes.
6. **Apply a reader-value and evidence gate.** For every candidate, state what reader-facing question it answers and what material misunderstanding or meaningful omission it could resolve. The article's silence alone is not evidence that information is missing, that an event occurred, or that an identity is correct. Do not invent facts, sources, relationships, or promising leads. Exclude generic requests to “add more detail,” speculative family connections, trivia, questions outside the article's scope, and gaps that would not materially improve understanding. Consider reader value, specificity of the clue, distinctness from other candidates, and whether the question can be answered independently. A listed holding is not automatically an article gap: sometimes the useful next question is what the source actually contains; sometimes the inventory is already serving readers well and needs no task.
7. **Create atomic tasks.** Create one task per independently answerable question, using `tasks/TEMPLATE.md` and the existing `tasks/research-*.md` naming and prose conventions. Each task must:
   - use a concise, question-shaped title and a unique descriptive kebab-case filename;
   - have frontmatter `type: ResearchTask`, `status: queued`, the current date, and a `targets` YAML list containing the target article path (plus another existing article only when the answer would genuinely inform it);
   - explain the reader/research value, cite the exact article passage or linked-note lead that prompted the question, and preserve uncertainty;
   - list only plausible search terms, collections, source types, or existing local leads, clearly as leads rather than evidence;
   - state one verifiable completion condition in `## Критерій завершення`.

   Preserve the template's sections and instructions. Do not claim that a source was inspected when it was not. Use `queued`: creating a task does not mean research has started. Do not create an umbrella task for independent questions or a task whose only deliverable is to improve wording.

8. **Check and apply navigation links separately from research gaps.** Prefer an Obsidian wikilink when a relevant local article exists. Link the existing first meaningful mention of that entity, preserving its displayed wording; do not change the surrounding prose or add factual claims. If a relevant entity (for example, a parish settlement) has no article in this site, it may be useful to link its existing mention to a confirmed Ukrainian-language Wikipedia article for navigation only. Never link to Russian-language Wikipedia. A narrow lookup may be used only to confirm that the exact Ukrainian-language page exists and refers to the same entity; do not use Wikipedia's content to support historical claims, cite it as evidence, or create a research task solely to add a navigation link. If the identity is ambiguous or no suitable page is confirmed, do not guess a URL or add a link. Avoid redundant links and do not link mentions in citations or source titles. Report the links actually applied; report an unapplied candidate only when it was worthwhile but could not be confirmed or safely resolved.
9. **Stay within scope.** Do not conduct substantive source research, rewrite article prose, change facts or citations, change existing task statuses or contents, create source records, or create articles. The only permitted article edits are navigation links as described above. Do not search the web for historical facts. You may inspect an obviously relevant existing task or source record to determine whether a proposed gap is already addressed, but do not represent that inspection as verification of its underlying historical source.
10. **Report clearly.** List every task created with its path and research question. Also list strong candidates already covered by an existing task, with that path; navigation links applied (target and local wikilink or confirmed Wikipedia destination); any worthwhile link candidate left unapplied and why; and briefly explain any worthwhile-looking research lead you excluded. When the article contains substantial archival or other research inventories, briefly name the distinct themes considered so the user can see that the review went beyond the first obvious gap; do not dump every raw clue or imply that every theme needs a task. If no task qualifies, say so and explain briefly; do not create a placeholder task.

## Task quality check

Before saving each task, verify that it has one answerable research question and one outcome; a concrete clue rather than an assumption; the correct article target(s); no duplicate in the queue; an honest `queued` status; and a completion condition that can be checked. A task is a research lead, not a finding, and its proposed source paths are not evidence until the source is actually examined.
